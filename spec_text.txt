Abstract
Natural-language commands are a practical interface for human-robot interaction, but
ambiguity still makes reliable execution difficult. In robot command interpretation,
ambiguity appears through indirect speech acts, missing references, fuzzy temporal or
quantitative language, and safety or capability constraints. Current approaches address
these ambiguity types in isolation, but they do not consistently coordinate routing when
multiple ambiguity types appear in a single command.
The proposal tests whether a risk-aware ambiguity manager can improve routing de-
cisions for compound ambiguous robot commands. The manager classifies ambiguity
type and task risk, then routes commands to one of several strategies: execute, clarify,
silently resolve, face preserving rejection, or multi step handling. Evaluation is con-
ducted on a labelled hybrid benchmark built from AmbiK, the Situational Awareness
for Goal Classification (SaGC) dataset, TEACh/TEACh-DA, and IndirectRequests, plus
a controlled manual compound-ambiguity extension. Performance is compared against
direct Large Language Model (LLM) interpretation and uniform baseline policies (al-
ways clarify, always resolve, and degree-based routing), with routing correctness as the
primary metric and ambiguity, clarification, intent/slot, and risk-sensitive metrics as
supporting measures.
Expected contributions are a routing-focused ambiguity taxonomy, a practical bench-
mark for compound ambiguity, and evidence on whether explicit type-and-risk-aware
coordination improves ambiguity-handling decisions compared with direct LLM parsing
and uniform policies in the robot command interpretation layer.

Chapter 1
Introduction
Natural-language instruction is useful for human-robot interaction because non-expert
users can use it in everyday terms. The trade-off is ambiguity. A single command
can include pragmatic indirection, missing references, vague temporal or quantitative
parameters, or implicit safety and feasibility assumptions [Zhang et al. 2025; Madureira
and Schlangen 2023; Sucker et al. 2024; Sucker and Henrich 2025]. In embodied
settings, ambiguity is not just a language nuisance: misinterpretation can cause task
failure, unsafe decisions, avoidable clarification loops, and reduced user trust [Scheutz
et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2025]. For that reason, ambiguity handling should be treated
as a decision problem about what to do next under uncertainty, not only as a parsing
problem.
Existing robotic natural language understanding systems handle several ambiguity forms
in isolation, including indirect speech acts, clarification requests, and fuzzy parameter
resolution [Zhang et al. 2025; Madureira and Schlangen 2023; Sucker et al. 2024;
Sucker and Henrich 2025]. For example, Indirect Speech Act (ISA) commands such as
“Can you pass that tool?” require pragmatic reinterpretation, while fuzzy commands
such as “Prepare some food in about ten minutes!” and quantity ranges such as “three to
eight arbitrary drinks” require parameter grounding [Zhang et al. 2025; Scheutz et al.
2022; Sucker et al. 2024; Sucker and Henrich 2025]. However, these mechanisms are
typically implemented as separate modules and do not adequately coordinate resolu-
tion when multiple ambiguity types co-occur in a single command. Existing meta-level
routing approaches are also typically driven by ambiguity degree thresholds rather than
explicit ambiguity-type and task-risk classification [Zhou et al. 2026].
The project evaluates a specific coordination problem: whether ambiguity-type and
risk-aware routing decisions are more appropriate than direct parsing or uniform han-
dling strategies for compound ambiguous robot commands. When pragmatic, refer-
ential, and temporal uncertainties combine, as in “Can you move that thing over there
in a few minutes?”, uncoordinated pipelines can produce repeated clarification loops or
unsafe silent assumptions because independently designed modules make incompatible
assumptions [Sarathy et al. 2025; Madureira and Schlangen 2023; Sucker et al. 2024;
Jackson 2022].

Research question. Does a risk-aware ambiguity manager improve routing decisions
for compound ambiguous robot commands compared to direct Large Language Model
(LLM) interpretation and uniform ambiguity-handling baselines, when evaluated using
ambiguity classification accuracy, routing correctness, clarification appropriateness, and
intent/parameter correctness on a labelled hybrid command benchmark?
Research aim and objectives.
This project aims to design and evaluate a routing
framework that classifies ambiguity type and task risk in robot commands, then se-
lects an appropriate resolution strategy. To achieve this, the project has the following
objectives:
1. Define an ambiguity taxonomy for robot command interpretation that aligns with
the methodology label schema.
2. Construct and harmonise a labelled hybrid command benchmark from AmbiK,
the SaGC dataset, TEACh/TEACh-DA, IndirectRequests, and a controlled man-
ual compound-ambiguity extension validated for ambiguity diversity and real-
ism [Ivanova et al. 2025; Park et al. 2024; Padmakumar et al. 2022; Mannekote
et al. 2024].
3. Implement representative baselines: direct LLM interpretation, always clarify (a
highly conservative policy), always silently resolve (a maximum-efficiency pol-
icy), and degree-based routing [Zhou et al. 2026].
4. Implement a type-and-risk-aware routing manager that selects strategy decisions
(execute, clarify, silently resolve, face preserving rejection, multi step).
5. Evaluate all systems using routing correctness as the primary metric, with sup-
porting ambiguity, clarification, intent and Core Propositional Content (CPC) cor-
rectness, and risk-sensitive metrics.
6. Analyse failures by ambiguity type, risk level, source dataset, and compound-
ambiguity status, and identify limitations for future embodied deployment.
Hypothesis. A routing layer that explicitly classifies ambiguity type and task risk be-
fore choosing a resolution strategy will yield higher routing correctness, specifically in
clarification, face-preserving rejection, and silently resolve decisions, than systems that
rely on a single uniform ambiguity-handling strategy.
Expected contribution. The project contributes:
• an explicit ambiguity taxonomy for routing-level command interpretation;
• a labelled hybrid command benchmark focused on routing decisions, including
controlled compound-ambiguity examples;
• a risk-aware routing framework that coordinates existing ambiguity-handling strate-
gies rather than replacing all downstream robot execution components;
• an empirical comparison against direct LLM interpretation and uniform ambiguity-
handling baselines using shared metrics and failure analysis.

Chapter 2
Background and Related Work
2.1
Natural Language Command Interpretation in Robotics
Natural-language robot interfaces improve accessibility, but they also expose interpre-
tation pipelines to ambiguity that can propagate into incorrect or unsafe behaviour if
it is not managed explicitly [Zhang et al. 2025]. Prior work has improved parsing and
grounding components, including symbolic-plus-LLM pipelines and direct LLM pars-
ing, but these systems are usually evaluated as interpretation modules rather than as
explicit strategy routers for downstream ambiguity handling [Sarathy et al. 2025].
That distinction matters here. The work does not test whether language can be parsed
at all; it tests whether routing decisions improve when ambiguity is classified by seman-
tic type and task risk before action selection. Integrated pipelines exist (for example, in-
tegrated architectures that combine pragmatic interpretation, reference resolution, and
norm/capability checks), but current evidence still points to the need for stronger ex-
plicit coordination when multiple ambiguity types interact in compound cases [Sarathy
et al. 2025].
2.2
Large Language Models for Structured Robotic Out-
puts
For routing-oriented robot command interpretation, LLMs are most useful when they
are constrained to produce machine-actionable outputs rather than free-form text [Sarathy
et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2026]. In practice, this means decoding into a fixed schema
(for example, JSON fields for intent, slots, ambiguity labels, risk level, and recom-
mended strategy), so outputs can be consumed directly by downstream modules with
predictable structure [Zhou et al. 2026].
This also motivates the prompting-versus-adaptation distinction. Prompt-only approaches
can work, but ambiguity-heavy settings often benefit from task-specific adaptation and
stricter output constraints when the system must follow a fixed decision protocol across
edge cases [Park et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2026; Sarathy et al. 2025].

2.3
Indirect Speech Acts (ISA) in Robot Commands
Indirect speech acts introduce a pragmatic mismatch between surface form and in-
tended action, so literal parsing alone can route commands incorrectly even when lex-
ical content is well-formed [Zhang et al. 2025]. Foundational speech-act and pragmat-
ics theories suggest that intended force is often inferred from context and cooperative
norms rather than literal syntax alone [Austin 1962; Searle 1969 1975; Grice 1975].
In robot settings, this is more than a semantic issue: ISA misinterpretation can degrade
teaming outcomes and produce brittle execution choices when directive intent is not
recovered before planning, including measured effects on trust and goal alignment in
physical collaboration [Zhang et al. 2025; Sarathy et al. 2025].
Related dialogue data work also indicates that natural task-oriented commands include
indirect user requests that require pragmatic inference rather than direct slot filling,
reinforcing ISA handling as a routing-relevant ambiguity type [Mannekote et al. 2024].
Figure 2.1: Interpreting a natural-language imperative requires identifying the speech
act (intent), core propositional content (new information), and supplemental proposi-
tional content (given knowledge used for reference resolution). Adapted from Sarathy
et al. [2025].
For this project, ISA handling motivates a dedicated ambiguity label and an explicit
routing decision point. Pragmatically indirect commands may need reinterpretation
before any clarification or parameter-grounding step is meaningful; otherwise later
modules can operate on the wrong intent structure and compound failure risk [Sarathy
et al. 2025]. This supports a sequencing view where ambiguity types are handled sep-
arately first, then coordinated through routing.

2.4
Clarification and Ambiguity Detection
Clarification literature shows that deciding when to ask is a separate problem from
deciding what a command means, and that this decision can be modeled either as ex-
plicit prediction or as thresholded escalation from internal resolution [Madureira and
Schlangen 2023; Zhou et al. 2026]. These methods demonstrate that clarification ap-
propriateness is measurable, but they are usually optimized around underspecification
severity and local confidence rather than a broader ambiguity-type portfolio. Embodied
dialogue benchmarks such as TEACh and DialFRED further motivate studying clarifica-
tion in multi-turn task contexts rather than single-turn utterances alone [Padmakumar
et al. 2022; Gao et al. 2022].
Figure 2.2: The ClarifyVC stage-wise generation flow. The schema-constrained pipeline
combines semantic parsing, adversarial construction, and multi-turn evolution to re-
solve ambiguous commands. Adapted from Zhou et al. [2026].
Work on morally sensitive interactions also shows that clarification and rejection are
coupled: reflexive clarification without adequate ethical filtering can itself be unde-
sirable in high-stakes requests [Jackson 2022]. This supports the proposal’s focus on
routing decisions that combine ambiguity interpretation with risk and capability con-
text.
That limitation is central to the proposal gap. A severity-first policy can improve detec-
tion for some fuzzy commands, yet commands with similar apparent ambiguity degree
can require different actions (e.g., reinterpretation for ISA vs. clarification for missing
referent vs. rejection for infeasibility) [Madureira and Schlangen 2023; Zhou et al.
2026]. ClarifyVC also defines ambiguity categories, but its operational decision bound-
ary is still largely severity-oriented for escalation, which motivates testing whether

explicit type-and-risk coordination improves routing on compound cases [Zhou et al.
2026].
2.5
Fuzzy Temporal and Quantitative Parameters
Fuzzy temporal and quantitative work shows that many vague commands can be re-
solved through formal internal grounding without immediate user interruption, using
satisfaction-based temporal models or globally constrained quantity grounding [Sucker
et al. 2024; Sucker and Henrich 2025]. These methods provide a technical basis for
the silently resolve route; in this proposal, that route is restricted to cases where
task risk is low enough for internal resolution to be acceptable. Compared with other
ambiguity categories, this area currently has relatively concentrated formal treatment
in the reviewed sources [Sucker et al. 2024; Sucker and Henrich 2025].
However, these methods are generally evaluated within their own ambiguity families
and assume upstream interpretation is already correct. They provide limited guidance
for cross-type coordination when fuzzy parameters co-occur with pragmatic or referen-
tial uncertainty in one command [Sucker et al. 2024; Sucker and Henrich 2025; Sarathy
et al. 2025]. This directly supports the proposal’s compound-ambiguity focus and the
need to test routing behaviour on multi-label cases rather than single-type examples
only.
2.6
Risk, Capability, and Transparent Rejection
Risk- and capability-aware work indicates that some commands should not be clarified
or silently resolved; they should be transparently rejected when authorization, feasibil-
ity, or normative constraints fail [Scheutz et al. 2022; Park et al. 2024; Jackson 2022].
This establishes rejection as a first-class strategy in command interpretation, not just an
execution-time exception.
Recent routing-style datasets and safety-oriented benchmarks further motivate separat-
ing infeasible or unsafe cases from merely ambiguous ones, because the communicative
objective changes from “recover missing information” to “justify non-execution” [Park
et al. 2024; Yin et al. 2024]. For this proposal, that evidence motivates explicit risk
and capability fields in the shared schema and risk-sensitive metrics such as safe re-
jection and unsafe silent-resolution rates. Uncertainty signaling is also central to this
decision. Prior routing work highlights two practical mechanisms: predictive-token un-
certainty when model probabilities are available, and context-sampling variance when
they are not [Park et al. 2024]. The context-sampling approach repeatedly queries the
model with controlled context permutations and measures output variance, providing
a model-agnostic uncertainty proxy for routing decisions [Park et al. 2024].

2.7
Synthesis and Research Gap
The literature supports a clear synthesis claim: robot command interpretation has cred-
ible mechanisms for multiple ambiguity categories, but these mechanisms are mostly
developed and evaluated in isolation rather than as a coordinated decision process for
compound commands [Sarathy et al. 2025; Madureira and Schlangen 2023; Sucker
and Henrich 2025]. Existing routing approaches provide useful threshold policies, yet
they are predominantly degree-based and do not fully operationalize type-specific coor-
dination across ISA, clarification, fuzzy grounding, and rejection under risk [Zhou et al.
2026]. The literature implicitly defines a hierarchical dependency between ambiguity
types (pragmatic →clarification →parametric), and existing systems partially opera-
tionalise aspects of this hierarchy, but do not explicitly coordinate ambiguity types and
task risk in a unified routing framework. Existing evaluations focus on isolated met-
rics rather than coordinated decision quality under compound ambiguity, motivating
routing correctness as the primary evaluation target.
This frames the project directly.
The open question is not whether each ambiguity
mechanism can work in isolation, but whether a type-and-risk-aware manager improves
routing decisions over direct LLM interpretation and uniform baselines when ambiguity
types co-occur [Sarathy et al. 2025; Park et al. 2024]. Dataset evidence shows progress
on ambiguity categorisation (for example, AmbiK’s ambiguity groupings), but still not
full coverage of the compound routing problem targeted here [Ivanova et al. 2025]. The
methodology addresses this by treating routing as the primary outcome and using a hy-
brid benchmark (AmbiK, SaGC, TEACh/TEACh-DA, IndirectRequests, plus a controlled
compound extension) with failure analysis by ambiguity type, risk level, and compound
status [Ivanova et al. 2025; Park et al. 2024; Padmakumar et al. 2022; Mannekote et
al. 2024]. By combining information-structure decomposition (Figure 2.1) with stage-

evaluate unified type-and-risk-aware routing against uniform baselines on compound
ambiguity cases. As summarised in Table 2.1, the literature highlights a clear divide
between current isolated capabilities and the missing meta-level coordination required

Chapter 3
Research Methodology
3.1
Research Design
The study uses a quantitative experimental design. The central comparison is whether
a type-and-risk-aware routing policy improves ambiguity-handling decisions compared
with direct LLM interpretation and uniform baselines on a labelled hybrid benchmark.
The research scope is intentionally limited to the natural-language coordination layer:
ambiguity classification, risk/capability assessment, and routing strategy selection. This
isolates the decision component under study and avoids confounding the evaluation
with perception, motion planning, and actuator-level failures. This scope is consistent
with prior evaluation approaches that separate interpretation quality from downstream
physical execution variability [Sarathy et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2026].
3.2
Proposed Risk-Aware Ambiguity Manager
The proposed method is a meta-level routing manager that coordinates ambiguity-
handling strategies rather than replacing all downstream robot subsystems. The man-
ager will be implemented as a supervised fine-tuned open-source LLM on the shared
hybrid benchmark, using schema-constrained decoding so each prediction is emitted
as structured JSON with ambiguity labels, risk/capability fields, and a routing deci-
sion [Park et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2026; Sarathy et al. 2025]. For each command, the
manager consumes:
• raw natural-language command;
• optional dialogue history;
• optional scene/context state;
• optional capability context.
9

--- PAGE BREAK ---
It produces:
• inferred intent and slot-level interpretation;
• ambiguity labels (single or multi-label);
• risk level and capability status;
• recommended strategy;
• optional clarification question or rejection explanation.
The routing strategy set is:
• execute
• clarify
• silently resolve
• face preserving rejection
• multi step
Strategy semantics are fixed during annotation and evaluation: execute routes a clear,
safe command directly to execution; clarify asks a targeted question to recover miss-
ing critical information; silently resolve internally grounds low-risk fuzzy parame-
ters without interrupting the user; face preserving rejection refuses unsafe or in-
feasible commands with a brief transparent explanation; and multi step applies an or-
dered strategy sequence for compound ambiguity. The term face preserving rejection
is used because the rejection response is designed to protect user trust and social dignity
while still communicating clear safety or feasibility constraints.
The manager treats compound ambiguity as a coordination problem.
It identifies
ambiguity type and stakes first, then routes to an action policy.
This framing fol-
lows prior work that treats indirect speech acts as pragmatic intent-recovery problems,
clarification as a separate decision process, fuzzy temporal/quantitative language as
parameter-grounding problems, and infeasible or unsafe instructions as explicit rejec-
tion or constraint-handling problems [Zhang et al. 2025; Madureira and Schlangen
2023; Zhou et al. 2026; Sucker et al. 2024; Sucker and Henrich 2025; Scheutz et al.
2022]. For uncertainty estimation, the manager will use context sampling with output-
variance scoring: the same command is evaluated across controlled permutations of
contextual input, and prediction instability is used as an uncertainty feature for escala-
tion and risk-sensitive routing [Park et al. 2024]. Figure 3.1 summarises the proposed
routing flow.

3.3
Dataset Strategy
The evaluation uses a hybrid benchmark built from datasets that cover different parts
of the routing problem. Representative source-backed examples include ISA commands
(“Can you pass that tool?”), fuzzy temporal commands (“Prepare some food in about ten
minutes!”), flexible quantity commands (“three to eight arbitrary drinks”, “one to four
blue gears”), and compound ambiguity commands (“Can you move that thing over there
in a few minutes?”) [Zhang et al. 2025; Sucker et al. 2024; Sucker and Henrich 2025;
Sarathy et al. 2025].
• AmbiK: ambiguity-focused robot tasks with ambiguous/unambiguous pairs and
clarification-relevant supervision [Ivanova et al. 2025].
• SaGC dataset: high-level goals with scene context and clear/ambiguous/infeasible
labels for routing evaluation (introduced with the CLARA framework) [Park et al.
2024].
• TEACh/TEACh-DA: embodied, multi-turn household dialogue with question-and-
answer behaviour and cooperative interaction that supports the study of mistake
recovery [Padmakumar et al. 2022].
• IndirectRequests: controlled indirect user requests for evaluating indirect speech
act handling [Mannekote et al. 2024].
• Manual compound extension: a controlled set of 50 targeted compound ambi-
guity examples for combinations underrepresented in public datasets.

No single source contains all labels required by this project’s taxonomy. A hybrid bench-
mark is therefore necessary because coverage is distributed across sources. To keep the
work feasible within the honours timeline, the benchmark will use a controlled sub-
set of approximately 250–400 examples selected from the core datasets, together with
a manually constructed extension of 50 targeted compound-ambiguity examples. The
project does not attempt exhaustive annotation of all source corpora; instead, examples
will be selected to balance source dataset, ambiguity type, risk level, and compound-
ambiguity status where possible.
SafeAgentBench is included as a targeted safety add-on for stress-testing rejection be-
haviour [Yin et al. 2024]. Dynamic-RDMM and ReferIt3D/RefCOCO/Collaborative Ma-
nipulation Corpus are reserved for augmentation and separate referential-grounding
sub-evaluations. The hybrid framing is consistent with reports that no single benchmark
fully covers the compound ambiguity categories and safety-sensitive routing decisions
required by this study.
3.4
Annotation Schema
All source examples will be converted into a shared JSONL schema for consistent train-
ing/evaluation interfaces. This schema is proposed for this project and is not presented
as complete in each original dataset. Core fields are:
• gold intent
• gold slots
• ambiguity present
• ambiguity types
• primary ambiguity type
• compound ambiguity
• compound ambiguity count
• risk level
• risk target
• capability status
• recommended strategy
• strategy sequence
• gold clarification question
• gold reinterpretation
• gold explanation
• success condition
12

--- PAGE BREAK ---
Several fields (especially risk-level targets, compound ambiguity labels/counts, and
strategy sequences) are not expected to be natively available across all source datasets
and will require explicit manual annotation under written guidelines. The methodol-
ogy therefore treats schema completion as a substantial annotation task, not a trivial
automatic mapping step. Mapping and annotation rules will be documented so prove-
nance is preserved and cross-dataset comparisons remain valid. Annotation decisions
will be calibrated on concrete command types: ISA (“Can you pass that tool?”), fuzzy
temporal (“Prepare some food in about ten minutes!”), fuzzy quantity (“three to eight
arbitrary drinks”), and compound ambiguity (“Can you move that thing over there in a
few minutes?”). To mitigate subjectivity in manual annotations, a rule-based annotation
guideline will be defined for ambiguity type, compound ambiguity identification, and
task risk classification. Where feasible, a subset of the dataset will be double-annotated
to assess inter-annotator agreement, and disagreements will be resolved through pre-
defined criteria. Inter-annotator agreement will be measured using metrics such as
Cohen’s kappa [Cohen 1960]. To improve reproducibility, the guideline will define
each label with operational criteria rather than only intuitive descriptions. For exam-
ple, ambiguity-type definitions will specify the minimum evidence required for assign-
ing pragmatic, referential, temporal, or quantitative categories, and will include short
borderline examples where multiple labels may be plausible. Compound-ambiguity an-
notation will require explicit justification for why a case is multi-label rather than a
single dominant ambiguity. Source provenance will be preserved for each example so
that evaluation can be stratified by originating dataset and annotation pathway. In ad-
dition, annotators will document concise reasons for assigned risk and recommended
strategy labels, enabling transparent review of difficult cases and more consistent dis-
agreement resolution.
3.5
Baseline Systems
Five systems will be compared:
1. Direct LLM interpretation: evaluates whether explicit routing logic is needed;
this corresponds to direct parser-style LLM command interpretation without a
dedicated coordination layer [Sarathy et al. 2025].
2. Always clarify (maximum conservatism): tests over-clarification and interac-
tion cost.
3. Always silently resolve (maximum efficiency): tests failure modes from aggres-
sive non-interruption, including unsafe guesses and brittle resolution behaviour
under severe ambiguity.
4. Degree-based routing: severity-threshold policy without explicit ambiguity-type
semantics, motivated by clarification-threshold style routing where stronger fuzzi-
ness triggers counter-questioning [Zhou et al. 2026].
5. Type-and-risk-aware routing (proposed): strategy chosen using ambiguity type,
risk, and capability together.

This baseline set spans heuristic policies and one threshold-style comparator, which
helps attribute gains to explicit coordination rather than implementation detail alone.
Clarification-first behaviour is motivated by clarification-decision literature [Madureira
and Schlangen 2023], while silent-resolution behaviour is motivated by fuzzy-parameter
grounding literature [Sucker et al. 2024; Sucker and Henrich 2025]. While always-
clarify and always-resolve represent intentionally extreme policies, they are included
to establish upper and lower behavioural bounds, with degree-based routing provid-
ing a stronger and more realistic comparison. The design isolates the marginal benefit
of type-and-risk-aware routing over direct parsing, threshold-only routing, and single-
policy heuristics.
3.6

The primary system-level metric is routing correctness, a project-defined measure of
whether the meta-manager selects the appropriate downstream handling path. It is
defined as the proportion of examples where predicted strategy matches gold recom-
mended strategy, and adapts strategy-selection ideas used in ambiguity-routing work
(for example, CLARA-style clear/ambiguous/infeasible classification and ClarifyVC es-
calation decisions) [Park et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2026].

• ambiguity classification micro-F1 and macro-F1;
• exact ambiguity-set match;
• clarification precision and clarification recall;
• intent correctness;
• slot binding correctness;
• risk-sensitive decision accuracy;
• safe rejection rate;
• unsafe silent-resolution rate.

Experimental Procedure
The procedure is:
1. Obtain and inspect core datasets.
2. Convert data into the shared schema.
3. Construct manual compound-ambiguity extension examples.
4. Create source-balanced development/evaluation partitions.
5. Implement all baselines.
6. Implement the proposed manager.
7. Run all systems on the same held-out evaluation set.
8. Compute primary and secondary metrics.
9. Conduct structured failure analysis.
All systems will be evaluated under the same schema and metric definitions so the

3.8
Failure Analysis
Failure analysis will identify where and why decisions fail. Errors will be grouped by:
• ambiguity type;
• compound versus non-compound ambiguity;
• risk level;
• source dataset;
• selected strategy;
• baseline category.
Special attention will be paid to unsafe silent-resolution and over-clarification patterns,
including ISA misrouting on “Can you pass that tool?” and compound-case breakdowns
on “Can you move that thing over there in a few minutes?”, and to whether failures
arise from upstream ambiguity/risk classification or downstream routing choice. This
failure-breakdown style is consistent with prior ambiguity-focused evaluation that sep-
arates detection, interpretation, and decision stages [Madureira and Schlangen 2023;
Sarathy et al. 2025].
Concretely, analysis will separate at least three error layers:
ambiguity-classification errors (wrong or incomplete labels), risk-classification errors
(misestimated stakes), and routing errors (incorrect strategy choice given predicted
labels). This decomposition helps identify whether performance limits come from rep-
resentation quality or policy quality. Results will also be compared across baselines
to identify baseline-specific failure patterns, such as over-clarification in conservative
policies or unsafe guesses in aggressive silent-resolution policies. Even if the proposed
manager does not outperform every baseline on every metric, these diagnostics are
still useful because they show which decision components generalise and which need
refinement in later routing-policy iterations.
3.9
Limitations and Ethical Considerations
This methodology is limited to text-first routing evaluation and does not directly mea-
sure embodied execution effects. Additional limitations include distributed dataset as-
sumptions, partial reliance on manually constructed compound examples, and possible
subjectivity in risk/capability labels when labels are inferred rather than provided.
No human-subject study is planned in the initial phase, so user-facing quality outcomes
are evaluated indirectly via routing and interpretation metrics. Public datasets will be
used according to licence terms, and manual examples will exclude sensitive personal
content. The ethical motivation is to reduce unsafe behaviour through explicit clarifi-
cation/rejection for risky or infeasible commands. This safety communication framing
is consistent with prior work on transparent rejection and justification in task-based
human-robot communication [Scheutz et al. 2022].

Chapter 5
Conclusion
Ambiguous natural-language robot commands are still hard to handle reliably when
different ambiguity types co-occur and require different response strategies.
The proposal addresses that gap by introducing a type-and-risk-aware ambiguity man-
ager that classifies ambiguity and task risk, then routes commands to execute, clarify,
silently resolve, face preserving rejection, or multi step handling. The evaluation plan
uses a labelled hybrid benchmark and compares the manager against direct LLM in-
terpretation and uniform handling baselines, with routing correctness as the primary
metric and ambiguity, clarification, intent/slot, and risk-sensitive measures as support-
ing evidence.
The expected contribution is a practical routing-focused framework and empirical ev-
idence on whether explicit ambiguity-type and risk-aware coordination improves de-
cision quality for ambiguous robot commands within a feasible honours-scale scope.
Feasibility is supported by the routing-only scope, controlled-subset benchmark design,
and reuse of shared baselines and metrics across all systems. These constraints keep
implementation and analysis bounded while preserving a meaningful empirical contri-
bution that can still be evaluated rigorously within honours project limits.
